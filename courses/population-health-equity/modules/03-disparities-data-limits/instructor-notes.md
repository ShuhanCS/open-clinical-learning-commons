# Instructor notes

## Teaching focus

The lesson is not a contest to find the largest ratio. Keep the population, denominator, age standard, reference group, missingness, support, and data-generating process visible beside every number.

Ask learners to explain why the same synthetic group can have a different rate difference and rate ratio under White, English, no reported disability, or overall reported references. A reference is an analytic choice, not a norm, ideal, biological baseline, or policy target.

## Required boundary

The combined race and ethnicity, language, and disability tables are separate generated margins. Learners may analyze each margin. They may not cross them, infer joint identities, describe intersectional disparities, or treat categories as fixed biological traits.

The reference statement may describe differences inside `fma-dp-01-equity-v1`. It must say that those differences were created by the generator. A learner who writes about real Massachusetts groups, observed cases, community deficits, eligibility, or allocation has crossed the module boundary.

## Suppression discussion

The source is synthetic and open, so the exercise does not claim that repository access is a real confidentiality control. The published tract-group table still enforces a realistic presentation rule: small cells are withheld, a lone suppressed cell receives complementary suppression, and no tract total is included that would expose a blank value by subtraction.

## Review prompts

- What changed when the reference changed?
- Which result is absolute, which is relative, and what decision could each distort?
- Who is absent before the analytic universe begins?
- Why does zero missing geography fail to prove complete capture?
- Which suppressed results remain unknowable in the publication table?
- Which result can be carried into a fictional checkpoint, and which claim must stop here?
